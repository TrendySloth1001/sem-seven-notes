**In simple words:** we add up the squared errors of the model $y = xw$ over all $N$ pairs, find the slope of that total with respect to $w$, and keep moving $w$ a small step against the slope until the error is smallest. <Cite id="gfg-gradient-descent" />

<Sum>

<Given>

$N$ pairs $(x_n, y_n)$, prediction $x_n w$, learning rate $\alpha$ (a small positive step size).

</Given>

<Find>

$E$, $\partial E / \partial w$ and the update rule.

</Find>

<Steps>

1. **Error of one pair:** $y_n - x_n w$. **Square it** so positive and negative errors cannot cancel, and **add over all pairs**. The $\dfrac{1}{2}$ is a convention that cancels the 2 from differentiating:

$$
E(w) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \left(y_n - x_n w\right)^2
$$

2. **Differentiate with the chain rule:** the outer square gives $2(y_n - x_n w)$, the inner part gives $-x_n$. Multiply by $\dfrac{1}{2}$ and sum:

$$
\frac{\partial E}{\partial w} = -\sum_{n=1}^{N} \left(y_n - x_n w\right) x_n
$$

3. **Step against the slope**, because the gradient points uphill:

$$
w \leftarrow w - \alpha\frac{\partial E}{\partial w} = w + \alpha\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(y_n - x_n w\right)x_n
$$

4. **Tiny example:** pairs $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 4)$, $w = 0$, $\alpha = 0.1$. Errors are 2 and 4, so $E = \dfrac{1}{2}(4 + 16) = 10$. Gradient $= -(2 \times 1 + 4 \times 2) = -10$. New $w = 0 - 0.1 \times (-10) = 1$. New errors are 1 and 2, so $E = \dfrac{1}{2}(1 + 4) = 2.5$.

</Steps>

<Answer>

$E = \dfrac{1}{2}\sum (y_n - x_n w)^2$ and $w \leftarrow w + \alpha \sum (y_n - x_n w)\,x_n$. In the example one step takes $w$ from 0 to 1 and $E$ from 10 to 2.5. <Cite id="gfdl-ch4" loc="§4.3" />

</Answer>

<Box kind="verify">

Expand $E$ for the example:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E &= \frac{1}{2}\big[(2 - w)^2 + (4 - 2w)^2\big] \\
&= 10 - 10w + 2.5w^2
\end{aligned}
$$

Its derivative $-10 + 5w$ equals $-10$ at $w = 0$, matching step 4. It is zero at $w = 2$, the true value (since $y = 2x$). Also $E(1) = 10 - 10 + 2.5 = 2.5$, matching.

</Box>

</Sum>

**Conclusion:** $w$ grows when predictions are too small and shrinks when they are too large. <Cite id="gfdl-ch8" /> <Cite id="wiki-gradient-descent" />
