**In simple words:** pooling makes a feature map smaller by summarizing each small window with one number. Max pooling keeps the largest value; average pooling keeps the mean. <Cite id="gfdl-ch9" />

**Why it is needed.** Fewer values mean less computation in later layers, and a small shift of a feature barely changes the output.

**How it works.** Choose a window (often 2×2) and a stride (often 2, so windows do not overlap). Slide it over each channel and write one value per window into the smaller output.

<Sum>

<Given>

a 2×2 window with the values 1, 3, 5 and 6.

</Given>

<Find>

the max-pooled and average-pooled output.

</Find>

<Steps>

1. **Max:** the largest value is 6.
2. **Average:** add the values, 1 + 3 + 5 + 6 = 15, then divide by the 4 values: 15 ÷ 4 = 3.75.

</Steps>

<Answer>

max pooling gives 6, average pooling gives 3.75.

</Answer>

<Box kind="verify">

Multiply back: 3.75 × 4 = 15, the sum of the values. Sorted, the values are 1, 3, 5, 6, so the maximum is 6.

</Box>

</Sum>

**Types:** max pooling (most common), average pooling, and global average pooling (a whole map becomes one number).

**Advantages:** a 2×2 window with stride 2 keeps a quarter of the values; tolerates small shifts; adds no learnable weights. **Disadvantage:** fine detail and exact position are lost. <Cite id="keras-maxpool2d" /> <Cite id="mlm-cnn-pooling" />

**Conclusion.** Pooling keeps the most important information in a smaller feature map.
