**In one line:** <mark>Collaborative filtering recommends items to a user using only the ratings of many users: it finds users with similar tastes and recommends what they liked</mark>. <Cite id="recommendation-systems-wiki-cf" /> <Cite id="recommendation-systems-google-cf" />

<Box kind="key">

**Key points to write (any collaborative filtering question):**

- <mark>People who agreed in the past will agree in the future</mark>; no knowledge of the items themselves is needed.
- Data: the <mark>utility matrix</mark>, users x items, ratings as cells, mostly empty (sparse).
- <mark>User-user CF: find similar users (cosine or Pearson), predict the rating as the similarity-weighted average of their ratings.</mark>
- <mark>Item-item CF: find items rated alike; used by Amazon.</mark>
- Prediction: $r_{xi} = \dfrac{\sum_y s_{xy} r_{yi}}{\sum_y s_{xy}}$ over the neighbours $y$.
- Limits: <mark>cold start (new users and items), sparsity, scalability</mark>.
- Difference from content-based: <mark>collaborative uses other users' ratings; content-based uses item properties</mark>.

</Box>

## Core answer (5 marks, fits every question here)

<Box kind="definition">

**Collaborative filtering (CF)** recommends items to a user based on what **other, similar users** liked. It does not need to know anything about the items, only who rated what. The idea: "people who agreed in the past will agree in the future". <Cite id="recommendation-systems-wiki-cf" /> <Cite id="recommendation-systems-google-cf" />

</Box>

**Why it is needed:** a shop such as Flipkart or Amazon sells millions of products, and no customer can browse them all. Recommending the right few increases sales and satisfaction. <Cite id="recommendation-systems-mmds-book" loc="§9.1" />

**Data used:** the **utility matrix**: rows are users, columns are items, cells are ratings. Most cells are empty because a user rates only a few items.

**Steps (user-user CF):** <Cite id="web-bda-2-mmds-recsys" loc="slides 22-27" />

1. Build the utility matrix from ratings, purchases and clicks.
2. Find the **similarity** of the target user with every other user (cosine, or Pearson correlation after subtracting each user's average).
3. Pick the most similar users (**neighbours**).
4. **Predict** the missing rating as the similarity-weighted average of the neighbours' ratings of the item.
5. **Recommend** the items with the highest predicted ratings.

**Item-item CF** does the same with similar items ("customers who bought this also bought that"): find items rated alike and average the user's own ratings of them. <Cite id="recommendation-systems-linden2003" />

```mermaid
%% caption: User-user collaborative filtering. Find similar users, take what they liked and recommend it to the target user.
flowchart LR
  U["Utility matrix: users x items"] --> S["Find users similar to the target user"]
  S --> N["Pick the nearest neighbours"]
  N --> L["Their ratings of items the user has not tried"]
  L --> R["Recommend the highest predicted items"]
```

**Conclusion:** <mark>collaborative filtering uses the crowd's ratings to predict what a user will like, and e-commerce and movie sites use it to suggest products</mark>.

## Extended answer (10 marks): add these

### 1. E-commerce example (Flipkart or Amazon)

(1 to 5 stars, `?` means not rated)

| User | Phone | Earbuds | Power bank | Laptop bag |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Asha | 5 | 4 | 5 | ? |
| Ravi | 5 | 5 | 4 | 5 |
| Meena | 1 | 2 | 1 | 2 |

Asha rates almost like Ravi (5, 4, 5 against 5, 5, 4) and very differently from Meena. So Ravi is Asha's neighbour. Ravi gave the laptop bag 5, so the site predicts a high rating for Asha and shows her the laptop bag. Meena's opinion gets little weight. <Cite id="recommendation-systems-gfg-python" />

### 2. Worked example: movie recommendation

<Sum>

<Given>

Ratings (1 to 5); "?" means not rated.

| | M1 | M2 | M3 | M4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| U1 | 5 | 3 | ? | 4 |
| U2 | 4 | 2 | 5 | 4 |
| U3 | 1 | 5 | 2 | 1 |

</Given>

<Find>

U1's predicted rating for M3, using cosine similarity on the movies both users rated.

</Find>

<Steps>

1. **Common movies** for U1 with U2 and with U3 are M1, M2, M4. U1 = (5, 3, 4), U2 = (4, 2, 4), U3 = (1, 5, 1).
2. **cos(U1, U2).** Dot product $= 20 + 6 + 16 = 42$. Lengths: $\sqrt{50} = 7.071$ and $\sqrt{36} = 6$. So $42 / (7.071 \times 6) = 0.990$.
3. **cos(U1, U3).** Dot product $= 5 + 15 + 4 = 24$. Length of U3 $= \sqrt{27} = 5.196$. So $24 / (7.071 \times 5.196) = 0.653$.
4. **Weighted average** of their M3 ratings (U2 gave 5, U3 gave 2): $(0.990 \times 5 + 0.653 \times 2) / (0.990 + 0.653) = 6.256 / 1.643 = 3.81$.

</Steps>

<Answer>

predicted rating of M3 for U1 $\approx 3.81$, so M3 is a reasonable recommendation (above the middle rating of 3).

</Answer>

<Box kind="verify">

The weights are 0.602 for U2 and 0.398 for U3, so $0.602 \times 5 + 0.398 \times 2 = 3.01 + 0.80 = 3.81$, the same value. It lies between 2 and 5 and nearer to 5, since U2 is more similar to U1.

</Box>

</Sum>

Raw-rating cosine ignores that some users rate high and others low; **Pearson correlation** (ratings minus each user's average) fixes this. <Cite id="recommendation-systems-wiki-pearson" />

### 3. Difference from content-based filtering

| Point | Content-based | Collaborative filtering |
|---|---|---|
| Uses | Item **properties** and the user's own history | **Ratings of many users** only |
| Idea | Items similar to what you liked | Users or items with similar rating patterns |
| Needs item features | Yes | No |
| Needs other users | No | Yes, many |
| New item | Can be recommended at once if its features are known | Cannot, until someone rates it |
| New user | Cannot (no profile) | Cannot (no history) |
| Variety | Low, over-specialises | Higher, can surprise |
| Example | Pandora matching song attributes | Amazon "customers who bought this also bought" |

<Cite id="recommendation-systems-gfg-cb" /> <Cite id="recommendation-systems-av" />

### 4. Advantages and limitations

| Advantages | Limitations |
|---|---|
| No item details needed | **Cold start:** new users and new items have no ratings |
| Finds surprising, new interests | **Sparsity:** few ratings per user |
| Works for any kind of item | Needs a lot of data; slow on huge matrices |

Real systems often combine both methods (**hybrid**).
