**In simple words:** tokenization cuts a text into small pieces called tokens (words or sentences). Stemming then chops each word down to a rough root so that related words match. <Cite id="text-preprocessing-gfg-tok" /> <Cite id="porter-stemmer-gfg-stemming" />

**Tokenization**

- A **token** is one unit of text, usually a word or a punctuation mark.
- Why needed: a computer sees only a long string of characters. It must first find the word boundaries before it can count, tag or parse words.
- Simple rule: split at spaces and punctuation. Hard cases: *don't* (do + n't?), *New York* (one unit or two?), *U.S.A.* (dots are not sentence ends). <Cite id="text-preprocessing-slp3-ch2" loc="§2.5" />

**Stemming**

- A **stem** is the part of a word left after removing suffixes. The result need not be a real word.
- Why needed: *run, runs, running* should count as the same word in search and classification.
- The Porter stemmer is the best known rule-based method. <Cite id="porter-stemmer-def" />

**Example** on "The runners were running quickly"

| Step | Output |
|---|---|
| Tokens | The, runners, were, running, quickly |
| After lowercase and stemming (Porter) | the, runner, were, run, quickli |

*running* became *run* and *runners* became *runner*. *quickli* is not a real word, which shows that a stem is only a rough root.

**Conclusion:** tokenization finds the words, stemming reduces their forms, and together they prepare text for later NLP steps.
