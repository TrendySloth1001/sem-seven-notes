**In simple words:** a language model gives probability zero to any word or n-gram it never saw in training, which ruins a whole sentence. **Good-Turing discounting** fixes this: it uses the things seen **once** to estimate how much probability the **unseen** things deserve, and lowers the counts of seen things a little to pay for it. <Cite id="ngram-language-models-wiki-gt" /> <Cite id="ngram-language-models-slp3-ch3" />

Let $N_c$ be the number of types seen exactly $c$ times and $N$ the total count of tokens.

$$
P_{GT}(\text{unseen}) = \frac{N_1}{N}, \qquad c^{*} = (c+1)\,\frac{N_{c+1}}{N_c}, \qquad P_{GT}(x) = \frac{c^{*}}{N}
$$

<Sum>

<Given>

a lake catch of 18 fish: carp 10, perch 3, whitefish 2, trout 1, salmon 1, eel 1. So $N = 18$, $N_1 = 3$, $N_2 = 1$.

</Given>

<Find>

the probability that the next fish is of a new kind, and the Good-Turing probability of trout.

</Find>

<Steps>

1. **Unseen mass.** $P(\text{new kind}) = N_1 / N = 3/18 = 1/6$. *Reason: three of the 18 catches were single appearances.*
2. **New count for trout.** $c^{*} = (1+1)\,N_2 / N_1 = 2 \times 1/3 = 2/3$. *Reason: the formula uses how many kinds were seen twice versus once.*
3. **Probability.** $P_{GT}(\text{trout}) = (2/3)/18 = 1/27 \approx 0.037$.

</Steps>

<Answer>

new kind: $1/6 \approx 0.167$; trout: $1/27 \approx 0.037$ (lower than the plain $1/18 \approx 0.056$).

</Answer>

<Box kind="verify">

Recompute step 3 another way: $(2/3)/18 = 2/54 = 1/27$. Trout dropped from $1/18$ to $1/27$, so some probability really moved to unseen kinds, as it should.

</Box>

</Sum>

**Limit:** the formula fails when $N_{c+1} = 0$ (here $N_4 = 0$, so perch cannot be adjusted); in practice the $N_c$ values are smoothed first. <Cite id="ngram-language-models-gale-sampson1995" />

**Conclusion:** Good-Turing re-estimates counts from the frequency of frequencies and gives the unseen events a fair share, $N_1/N$.
