**In simple words:** an n-gram model gives probability zero to a word sequence it never saw, which ruins a whole sentence. Smoothing takes a little probability from seen sequences and gives it to unseen ones. Good-Turing is one such method. <Cite id="class-nlp-m6" loc="slide 44" />

The class names Good-Turing smoothing but gives no formula, so the idea below is the standard one.

- Words seen c times get a lower count $c^* = (c+1)\,N_{c+1}/N_c$, where $N_c$ is the number of different words seen exactly c times.
- The total probability given to unseen words is $N_1/N$, where $N_1$ is the number of words seen once and $N$ the number of tokens.

| Quantity | Value in the example |
|---|---|
| Words seen once, twice, three times ($N_1$, $N_2$, $N_3$) | 3, 1, 1 |
| Tokens $N = 3 \times 1 + 1 \times 2 + 1 \times 3$ | 8 |
| Probability for all unseen words $= N_1/N$ | 3/8 = 0.375 |
| Adjusted count for a word seen once $= 2 \times N_2/N_1$ | 2 x 1/3 = 0.67 |

**Conclusion:** Good-Turing uses the number of words seen once to estimate the chance of unseen ones, so no sequence gets probability zero.
