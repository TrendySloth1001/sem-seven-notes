**In simple words:** a **stemmer** cuts the endings off words so that *connect, connected, connecting, connection* all become one stem, *connect*. The **Porter stemmer** (M. F. Porter, 1980) does this with a fixed list of suffix-stripping rules applied in five ordered steps. It does not use a dictionary, and the stem need not be a real word (*happy* becomes *happi*). <Cite id="porter-stemmer-def" /> <Cite id="porter-stemmer-gfg-stemming" />

**Why it is needed:** search engines and text classifiers want related word forms to count as one word, which shrinks the vocabulary and improves recall.

**Basic idea: the measure $m$.** Write a word as `[C](VC)^m[V]`, where C is a run of consonants and V a run of vowels (in Porter's rule *y* counts as a vowel after a consonant). $m$ counts the VC pairs and says how long the stem is. A rule fires only if the stem left after cutting is long enough.

| $m$ | Examples |
|---|---|
| 0 | tr, ee, tree, by |
| 1 | trouble, oats, trees, ivy |
| 2 | troubles, private, oaten, orrery |

Conditions used: `*v*` (stem has a vowel), `*d` (ends in a double consonant), `*o` (ends consonant-vowel-consonant, last not w, x or y).

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  A["Word"] --> B["1a: plurals"]
  B --> C["1b, 1c: -ed, -ing, -y"]
  C --> D["2: long suffixes to short, m>0"]
  D --> E["3: -ical, -ful, -ness, m>0"]
  E --> F["4: remove suffix, m>1"]
  F --> G["5: tidy final -e and -ll"]
  G --> H["Stem"]
```

**The steps with sample rules:**

| Step | Job | Sample rules | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1a | plurals | SSES to SS; IES to I; S removed | caresses to caress; ponies to poni |
| 1b | -ed, -ing (stem needs a vowel) | ED, ING removed; then AT to ATE, or fix double letters | motoring to motor; hopping to hop; filing to file |
| 1c | y to i (stem has a vowel) | Y to I | happy to happi |
| 2 | double suffixes, $m>0$ | ATIONAL to ATE; IZATION to IZE; FULNESS to FUL | relational to relate |
| 3 | $m>0$ | ALIZE to AL; FUL, NESS removed; ICATE to IC | hopeful to hope |
| 4 | remove suffix, $m>1$ | AL, ANCE, ER, IC, ABLE, MENT, ION, ISM, ATE, IVE, IZE removed | general to gener |
| 5 | final e if $m>1$ (or $m=1$ and not `*o`); -ll to -l if $m>1$ | E removed | relate to relat |

<Sum>

<Given>

the words *generalizations* and *hopefulness*.

</Given>

<Find>

their Porter stems, step by step.

</Find>

<Steps>

1. ***generalizations*, step 1a:** ends in S, so remove it: *generalization*.
2. **Step 2:** IZATION to IZE, stem *general* has $m = 3 > 0$: *generalize*.
3. **Step 3:** ALIZE to AL, stem *gener* has $m = 2 > 0$: *general*.
4. **Step 4:** AL removed, stem *gener* has $m = 2 > 1$: *gener*. Step 5 has nothing to do.
5. ***hopefulness*, step 1a:** ends in SS, which stays SS. Steps 1b and 1c do not apply.
6. **Step 2:** FULNESS to FUL, stem *hope* has $m = 1 > 0$: *hopeful*.
7. **Step 3:** FUL removed, stem *hope* has $m = 1 > 0$: *hope*.
8. **Step 5a:** the stem *hop* has $m = 1$ and ends consonant-vowel-consonant (`*o`), so the final E is kept: *hope*.

</Steps>

<Answer>

*generalizations* gives *gener*; *hopefulness* gives *hope*.

</Answer>

<Box kind="verify">

Running the original Porter algorithm in NLTK <Cite id="porter-stemmer-nltk-stem" /> gives *gener* and *hope* for these two words. Measure check: *gener* = g-e-n-e-r is C V C V C, so (VC)(VC) and $m = 2$.

</Box>

</Sum>

**Advantages:** fast, simple, no dictionary, widely used. **Disadvantages:** over-stemming (*university* and *universe* both become *univers*), under-stemming, and non-words as stems. Lemmatization or the Snowball stemmer can be used instead.

**Conclusion:** Porter's stemmer strips suffixes in five rule steps, guarded by the measure $m$, to reduce related words to one stem.
