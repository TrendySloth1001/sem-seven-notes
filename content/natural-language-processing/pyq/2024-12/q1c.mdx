**In simple words:** a single sentence is rarely the whole story. **Discourse analysis** looks at how sentences join to form a meaningful text. **Pragmatic analysis** looks at what the speaker really means in a situation, beyond the dictionary meaning. These are the last two levels of understanding in NLP. <Cite id="nlp-introduction-gfg-phases" />

**Discourse analysis.** The meaning of a sentence depends on the sentences before it. Two things are studied: **coherence** (do the sentences connect logically?) and **reference** (what do pronouns like *he*, *it* point to?). <Cite id="discourse-reference-slp3-disc" /> <Cite id="discourse-reference-wiki-discourse" />

Example: "Ravi went to the shop. **He** bought milk." Discourse analysis links *He* to *Ravi*. Without this link the second sentence is unclear.

**Pragmatic analysis.** It uses real-world knowledge, the speaker's intention and the situation to find the intended meaning. <Cite id="discourse-reference-wiki-pragmatics" />

Example: "Can you pass the salt?" The literal meaning is a question about ability, but the intended meaning is a request. Another: "It is cold here" may mean "please close the window".

| Point | Discourse analysis | Pragmatic analysis |
|---|---|---|
| Looks at | Links between sentences | Meaning in context and intention |
| Key problem | Pronoun and reference resolution, coherence | Intended meaning, indirect requests |
| Example | He = Ravi | "Can you pass the salt?" = a request |

**Conclusion:** discourse analysis joins sentences into a whole text, and pragmatic analysis decides what the speaker actually wants to say.
