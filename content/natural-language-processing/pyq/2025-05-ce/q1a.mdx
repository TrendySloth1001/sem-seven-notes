**In simple words:** morphology studies how words are built from smaller parts (morphemes). **Inflectional** morphology changes a word only to fit the grammar, so it stays the same word. **Derivational** morphology builds a new word with a new meaning, often in a new word class. <Cite id="morphology-fst-wiki-inflection" /> <Cite id="morphology-fst-wiki-derivation" /> <Cite id="morphology-fst-gfg-morph" />

**Examples:** `cat` to `cats` (plural) and `walk` to `walked` (past tense) are inflection. `happy` to `happiness` (adjective to noun) and `teach` to `teacher` (verb to noun) are derivation.

| Point | Inflectional | Derivational |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Shows grammar: number, tense, person, case | Makes a new word with a new meaning |
| Word class | Does not change (noun stays noun) | Often changes (verb to noun) |
| Meaning | Same core meaning | New or changed meaning |
| Affixes in English | Few and fixed: `-s`, `-ed`, `-ing`, `-er`, `-est` | Many: `un-`, `re-`, `-ness`, `-able`, `-ment` |
| Position | Outermost, after derivational suffixes | Closer to the stem |
| How regular | Very regular, applies to almost every word | Irregular, applies to some words only |
| Dictionary | Forms share one entry (lemma) | Gets its own entry |
| Example | `play` to `plays`, `playing` | `play` to `player`, `playful` |

Order inside a word: derivation sits next to the stem and inflection goes last, as in `nation` + `-al` + `-ize` + `-ed` (`nationalized`). <Cite id="morphology-fst-jm-ch3-notes" />

**Use in NLP:** lemmatizers undo inflection to find the lemma; stemmers cut both kinds.

**Conclusion:** inflection changes the form of a word for grammar, derivation changes the word itself.
