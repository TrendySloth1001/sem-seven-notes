**In simple words:** ambiguity means a text can be understood in more than one way. In **lexical ambiguity** the confusion comes from one word. In **syntactic ambiguity** it comes from the structure of the whole sentence. <Cite id="ambiguity-challenges-gfg-ambiguity" /> <Cite id="ambiguity-challenges-tp-ambiguities" />

**Lexical example:** "I went to the **bank**" (money or river?). Also *book* can be a noun ("read a book") or a verb ("book a ticket"). <Cite id="ambiguity-challenges-wiki-polysemy" />

**Syntactic example:** "I saw the man with a telescope." Either I used the telescope to see him, or the man had the telescope. The words are clear; the way they attach is not. <Cite id="ambiguity-challenges-wiki-synamb" />

| Point | Lexical ambiguity | Syntactic ambiguity |
|---|---|---|
| Source | One word with many meanings or tags | Many possible sentence structures |
| Also called | Word-level, semantic | Structural, grammatical |
| Cause | Homonymy, polysemy, noun/verb words | Phrase attachment, conjunction scope |
| Example | "bank", "bat" | "old men and women" |
| Parse trees | Often the same tree, with a different word meaning (a noun/verb word can change the tree) | Two or more different trees |
| Resolved by | POS tagging, word sense disambiguation | Probabilistic parsing, context |

**Conclusion:** lexical ambiguity is about what a word means, syntactic ambiguity is about how words group; they need different tools to resolve.
